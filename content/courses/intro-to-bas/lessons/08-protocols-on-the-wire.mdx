A BAS is, at the end of the day, a lot of small computers talking to each other. The protocols that let them talk are the closest thing the industry has to a shared language, and learning the basics of how those protocols work changes how the whole field looks.

This lesson is deliberately broad and shallow. Each of the protocols below has its own course's worth of detail; the goal here is to put names on the shapes and to understand why you reach for one over another.

<LessonSection title="Why protocols exist" level={2}>

In the pneumatic era, signals were carried by air pressure in tubes. Every manufacturer's controller could read every other manufacturer's signals because pressure is pressure. When the industry went digital in the 1980s and '90s, that compatibility vanished. Each vendor had its own wire format, its own value encoding, and its own way of describing what a "zone temperature" was.

A **protocol** is the rule book that lets two devices, possibly from different vendors, agree on:

- How the bits are framed on the wire (the physical and data-link layer)
- How addresses identify devices
- What an "object" or "register" or "point" looks like
- How a device asks for a value or commands a change
- What errors mean

Protocols don't make systems interoperable on their own, they make interoperability *possible*. There is still plenty of integration work after the protocol is agreed on.

<Callout variant="info">
  When the industry says "open protocol," it usually means a standard a vendor
  doesn't own outright, BACnet, Modbus, LonWorks, OPC UA, KNX, MQTT. A vendor
  can be BACnet-compliant and still have proprietary features that only their
  controllers understand. "Open" is a spectrum.
</Callout>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="A BACnet primer" level={2}>

Because BACnet is everywhere in modern commercial BAS, it's worth understanding its mental model at least at the surface. The core idea: a BAS device is a bag of <KeyTerm term="objects" definition="Named things inside a BACnet device, Analog Input 1, Binary Output 4, Schedule 3, Trend Log 12. Every object has properties (Present_Value, Status_Flags, Units, etc.) that other devices can read or write." />, each of which has <KeyTerm term="properties" definition="Named attributes of a BACnet object. The two most important are Object_Identifier and Present_Value. Other common properties include Status_Flags, Units, Description, and (for I/O) Out_Of_Service." />, accessed through <KeyTerm term="services" definition="Actions one BACnet device performs on or asks of another. ReadProperty and WriteProperty are the most common. SubscribeCOV, ConfirmedEventNotification, WhoIs/IAm are the other early ones to recognize." />.

### IP vs MS/TP

The two BACnet variants you'll see on most projects are BACnet/IP and BACnet MS/TP. They carry the same protocol on different physical media.

<LessonSection title="Networks and the IT conversation" level={2}>

A new wrinkle for BAS work in the last decade: building automation networks are now expected to coexist with corporate IT.

That means <KeyTerm term="VLAN" definition="Virtual LAN. A logical network segment that shares physical switches with other segments but is isolated at the data-link layer. BAS networks are increasingly placed on their own VLAN, separate from corporate user traffic." /> segmentation, firewall rules, identity management, certificate handling, and patch cycles, all topics that used to belong to the corporate IT team and now show up on BAS submittals. The protocols themselves haven't changed much, but the *network they ride on* has become a shared resource.

<Callout variant="warning">
  Do not plug a BAS controller into a corporate network without coordinating
  with IT. A controller sending broadcast discovery traffic can saturate a
  segment, and a corporate firewall blocking BACnet UDP traffic can silently
  break a building. This is one of the most common
  finger-pointing moments on a modern project.
</Callout>

<Callout variant="industry-insight">
  The BAS engineers who will be most valuable over the next decade are the ones
  who can hold a credible conversation with IT, VLANs, certificates, change
  management, network monitoring. The mechanical knowledge still matters; it
  just isn't enough on its own anymore.
</Callout>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="Gateways, when nothing matches" level={2}>

Sometimes a piece of equipment speaks one protocol and the BAS speaks another. The solution is a **protocol gateway**, a small box (or a service running on a supervisor) that translates between the two.

A common example: a chiller that speaks Modbus exposes a few dozen registers; the BAS is BACnet-centric. A gateway reads the Modbus registers and re-presents them as BACnet objects. The BAS sees an ordinary BACnet device; the chiller sees an ordinary Modbus master.

<RevealCard
  front="Why gateways are a hassle"
  back="They add a translation layer that has to be configured, maintained, and updated when either side changes. A failed gateway is a single point of failure that hides upstream of every point it carries. Veteran integrators avoid them when they can, but a building with one BACnet-incompatible chiller, two LonWorks lighting controllers, and a Modbus power meter rack will absolutely have several."
/>

</LessonSection>
