<LessonSection title="How to learn this for real" level={2}>

The intro course is reading. The work isn't. A few habits make BAS skill compound faster than just doing the next course. Two worth naming explicitly: read finished <KeyTerm term="sequences of operations" definition="The narrative behavioral spec for every system the BAS controls. Reading two or three finished sequences end-to-end is the fastest way to learn how the field thinks." />, and sit through every <KeyTerm term="commissioning walk" definition="A structured site visit where the cx agent exercises the sequence of operations on real equipment. The single highest-density learning environment in the industry." /> you can find your way into.

<Comparison
  leftLabel="Easy to skip"
  rightLabel="Worth doing anyway"
  rows={[
    { label: "Read someone else's sequence of operations", left: "Why? You'll write your own.", right: "Reading two or three real, finished sequences end to end is the fastest way to learn how the field thinks." },
    { label: "Watch a commissioning walk", left: "Boring, slow, lots of clipboard work.", right: "It's where every assumption from design meets reality. Sit in on as many as you can." },
    { label: "Open a panel door", left: "Looks complicated. Wires everywhere.", right: "It stops looking complicated as soon as you've terminated one yourself. The fastest way to lose fear of a panel is to wire one." },
    { label: "Read a trend before drawing a conclusion", left: "Faster to ask a sensor.", right: "The trend tells you more than the live value. It's also what saves your conclusion from being wrong." },
    { label: "Talk to the operator", left: "They aren't on the engineering team.", right: "They've been watching the building for years. Half the bugs you'll chase are bugs they already know about." },

<Callout variant="tip">
  Pick one platform and learn it deeper than your job requires. Pick one
  protocol and read its specification at least once, cover to cover. Pick one
  building type and learn its sequences inside out. In ten years, those three
  picks will define the career you have.
</Callout>

<Aside attribution="A senior BAS practitioner, asked what they'd tell themselves at year zero">
  Don't try to know everything. Be a person who can find out anything. The
  field is too big for the first; everyone good at this is great at the second.
</Aside>

<LessonSection title="A small send-off" level={2}>

A BAS isn't a product. It's a long argument, conducted in panels and pipes and trend logs and sequence narratives, about how a building should behave. The argument never really ends. Every season pushes back; every operator has a different idea; every new tenant changes the load; every new protocol changes what's possible. Working in BAS means becoming comfortable with that, and finding the satisfaction in it.

Whichever course you pick up next, the same five jobs and six roles will be in the background. Once you've seen them, you can't unsee them.

Go build something.

</LessonSection>
