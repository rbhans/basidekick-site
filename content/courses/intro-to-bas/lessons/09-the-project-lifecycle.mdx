Lesson 5 introduced the rough shape of a project, design, bid, submittals, construction, commissioning, turnover. This lesson treats each of those phases with more care and names the documents that hold them together.

A BAS project is, more than anything, a *paper trail*. The actual controllers and wires are the easy part. The hard part is the agreement about what those controllers should do, what they're allowed to cost, who's responsible when something doesn't work, and what gets handed off at the end. Every phase below produces a document or two whose only job is to pin one of those agreements in place.

<LessonSection title="Phase 1: pre-design" level={2}>

Before any engineer drafts a sequence, the owner answers a few questions: what is this building for, who will use it, how much can it cost, and what does "good" look like a year after move-in.

The output of pre-design is usually a document called the **OPR**, the <KeyTerm term="Owner's Project Requirements" definition="A document, usually written by the owner or a consultant, that captures the owner's intended use, comfort requirements, energy targets, sustainability goals, and acceptance criteria for a project. The OPR is the anchor every later phase ought to trace back to." />. Strong OPRs cite measurable acceptance criteria; weak ones say "comfortable" without defining for whom.

<Callout variant="industry-insight">
  Commissioning agents read the OPR first when they show up on a project. If it
  doesn't exist, or it's a single page of marketing copy, they know the project
  has a foundation problem before they've opened a panel.
</Callout>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="Phase 2: design" level={2}>

The design team, usually a mechanical engineer at an MEP firm, turns the OPR into a technical specification. Three documents anchor the BAS scope, the <KeyTerm term="BOD" definition="Basis of Design, narrative explaining why the design selections were made. Why VAV instead of fan-coil? Why dual chillers instead of a single? The BOD answers the 'why' the drawings don't." />, the sequence of operations, and the point list.

<RevealCard
  front="Basis of design (BOD)"
  back="The narrative document explaining the design intent behind the system selections. Why a VAV system instead of a fan-coil approach? Why a dual-chiller plant instead of a single? The BOD answers the 'why' that the drawings don't."
/>

<RevealCard
  front="Sequence of operations (SOO)"
  back="The behavioral specification for every system the BAS controls. Reads almost like code, 'when outside air temperature is above 65°F, enable the economizer and reset the discharge-air setpoint per the cooling demand schedule.' The closest thing to a spec the BAS will ever have."
/>

<RevealCard
  front="Point list"
  back="A tabular spec listing every BAS point, sensor, output, calculated value, grouped by equipment. Each row names the point, the type (AI, AO, BI, BO, BV, AV), the units, and any alarms or trends that should apply to it. The point list is what the integrator prices."
/>

The BAS engineer rarely participates in this phase directly, but the *quality* of these documents will dictate how the rest of the project goes. Anyone moving into a design role should plan to spend months reading well-written examples.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="Phase 3: bid and award" level={2}>

The owner sends the design package out for pricing. Integrators bid; the owner selects one. The contracting model, whether it's design-bid-build, design-build, or progressive design-build, determines who is on the hook for what.

    { label: "Common on", left: "Public projects, traditional commercial work", right: "Tech campuses, hospitals, mission-critical work" },
  ]}
  caption="Different delivery models mean different muscles. A BAS engineer who can work in both has a much longer runway."
/>

The bid phase produces an **awarded contract** and, just as important, a set of **bid clarifications**, written exceptions, alternates, and assumptions the integrator made when pricing. Those clarifications become contract documents.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="Phase 4: submittals" level={2}>

Once the contract is signed, the integrator produces a stack of documents the owner and design team have to approve before any wire gets pulled. These are the **submittals**.

<Callout variant="tip">
  Submittals are the last cheap place to catch a design problem. After this
  phase, fixes cost real money, controllers ordered, panels built, schedule
  committed. A BAS engineer who reads submittals carefully saves their project
  manager hours of pain later.
</Callout>

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="Phase 5: construction" level={2}>

Wires get pulled. Conduit gets bent. Panels get assembled in a shop and shipped to site. Controllers get mounted in mechanical rooms or above ceilings. Equipment shows up on the dock and finds its place. This is the longest phase by calendar.

The BAS engineer's day during construction is mostly:

- Coordinating with the mechanical contractor, when a coil gets installed, where the sensor goes, who is responsible for power to a damper actuator
- Walking the site to confirm field reality matches the drawings
- Writing <KeyTerm term="RFIs" definition="Request for Information. A formal question to the design team when something on site doesn't match the drawings or the spec. The RFI response becomes part of the contract record." /> when something doesn't match, a question to the design team asking for clarification or a redirect
- Catching late-stage scope changes (an **ASI**, additional service info, from the architect)

A BAS panel finishing programming in the shop, then waiting six weeks for the mech contractor to finish piping a fan coil, is the standard rhythm.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="Phase 6: commissioning" level={2}>

Now the BAS gets pressure-tested. **Commissioning** (often shortened to **cx**) is the structured process of verifying that the system behaves the way the design said it would. There are roughly four stages, pre-functional checks, point-to-point verification, the <KeyTerm term="functional performance test (FPT)" definition="System-by-system walks of the sequence of operations. Cx agent forces each scenario the SOO describes and confirms the BAS does what the document says." />, and the <KeyTerm term="integrated systems test (IST)" definition="Multi-system scenarios, smoke control, fail-safe on power loss, fire-alarm coordination. Where seams between trades show up." />.

<RevealCard
  front="Pre-functional checks"
  back="Each device gets inspected before it's powered on. The commissioning agent or the integrator walks every panel, every controller, every sensor, and confirms they're installed, addressed, and ready to commission. A pre-functional checklist is the lowest level of cx documentation."
/>

<RevealCard
  front="Point-to-point checks (P2P)"
  back="For each BAS point, the team forces a known input and confirms the BAS sees the expected output. A zone temperature sensor reads 72°F when the room is at 72°F. A damper goes 0% closed when the BAS commands 0%. Tedious; non-negotiable."
/>

<RevealCard
  front="Functional performance tests (FPT)"
  back="System-by-system tests of the sequence of operations. The cx agent walks each scenario the SOO describes, economizer enable, freeze-stat response, schedule transition, alarm fire, and confirms the BAS does exactly what the document says it should."
/>

<RevealCard
  front="Integrated systems test (IST)"
  back="Multi-system scenarios. A fire-alarm test that ought to trigger smoke control. A loss-of-power event that should fail-safe across HVAC, lighting, and security. These tests are where any miscommunication between trades surfaces."
/>

<Aside attribution="A commissioning agent with two decades of FPT walks">
  The functional test is where every bad assumption from the past year shows up.
  If you're an integrator, the goal isn't to pass, it's to find the broken
  thing while the painters are still on site, before the owner is paying utility
  bills.
</Aside>

The end of commissioning produces a **cx report** that the owner accepts (or doesn't). Punch lists from this phase often run a hundred items deep on a substantial project.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection title="Phase 7: turnover and warranty" level={2}>

Construction is over. The cx report is signed. Now the BAS engineer's audience changes from designers and contractors to *operators*.

    },

    },
    {
      value: "warranty",

            Typically a one-year warranty period during which the integrator returns to fix anything that wasn't a maintenance issue. Smart integrators schedule a 9-month tuning walk, sequences that worked in moderate spring weather almost always need adjustment by the time the building has been through a full summer and winter.
          </p>
        </div>
      ),
    },
  ]}
/>

</LessonSection>
